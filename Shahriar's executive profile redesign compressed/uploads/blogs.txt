[ListParagraph] Still Running a 2010 Playbook? Your Customers Have Already Left.
Customer behaviour has changed permanently. Most transformation strategies haven't caught up.
There is a quiet assumption I still encounter in boardrooms: that customer expectations evolve gradually, and that a strong brand built over decades provides enough cushion to adapt at a comfortable pace.
That assumption is no longer safe.
Customer behaviour has not evolved gradually over the past decade — it has reset entirely. The pandemic compressed years of digital adoption into months. AI-driven personalization, instant fulfilment, and on-demand everything have rewired what customers consider normal. And the companies still operating on a 2010-era model of customer engagement — even strong, profitable ones — are accumulating a kind of structural risk that does not show up in quarterly results until it is too late to reverse.
The Expectation Gap Is Wider Than Most Leaders Realise
A decade ago, a customer might tolerate a multi-step booking process, a delayed response, or a one-size-fits-all service experience, because the alternative was calling a competitor who offered roughly the same thing.
That competitive cushion is gone.
Today's customer compares every experience — booking a rental car, opening a bank account, requesting support — against the best digital experience they had that morning, regardless of industry. If a ride-hailing app gets them a car in ninety seconds with full price transparency, that becomes the implicit benchmark for every other service interaction in their life, including yours.
I saw this directly in the mobility sector. For years, the car rental industry operated on processes designed for a pre-smartphone customer: paperwork-heavy counters, opaque pricing, and limited self-service. It worked, until it didn't. Newer entrants — leaner, digital-first, built around instant booking and radical transparency — didn't out-market the incumbents. They simply met customers where customer expectations had already moved to.
The uncomfortable lesson I took from this: the threat rarely announces itself as a competitor doing something dramatically better. It looks, at first, like a slightly more convenient alternative that customers quietly start preferring.

I have sat in strategy reviews where solid financial performance was used as evidence that transformation could wait. I understand the instinct — when the numbers are healthy, urgency is hard to manufacture.
But healthy numbers today are a lagging indicator, not a forward signal. Customer loyalty built on habit and switching cost is eroding faster than most companies' internal metrics can detect, because digital-native competitors have made switching nearly frictionless.
The companies most at risk are not the ones performing badly. They are the ones performing adequately, with leadership teams who interpret stability as proof that transformation is optional rather than overdue.

"Digital transformation" has become a phrase so overused that it risks losing meaning. Having watched this play out up close, I've come to believe most transformation failures aren't about technology gaps at all. They're about five specific, less comfortable things.
Decision speed, not technology adoption. This is the one most companies get backwards. They buy AI tools, new CRMs, automation platforms — and then route every decision through the same five committees they had in 2010. The technology becomes a faster horse pulling the same slow cart. The real fix is pushing decision rights down to the people closest to the customer, and shrinking the number of approvals needed to act on what customers are saying right now.
Personalization at the point of interaction, not in a quarterly campaign. Customers no longer separate marketing from product from service — they expect the company to already know them at every touchpoint. Most legacy companies still personalize in batches, like a targeted email campaign, rather than in the moment, like an app adjusting what it shows you right now. This is the most visible gap between digital-native and legacy competitors.
Radical transparency as a trust mechanism. This one is underrated. Customers today distrust opacity by default — hidden fees, vague terms, "contact us for pricing." The companies winning right now are over-explaining: real-time pricing, visible inventory, clear timelines. Transparency has quietly become a competitive advantage, not just an ethical nice-to-have.
Treating churn signals as urgent, not as a monthly report. Most companies still review customer attrition retrospectively, as a slide in a monthly business review. By the time it's on a slide, you've already lost the customer — and probably ten others like them. What matters is building systems that flag dissatisfaction in real time and route it to someone empowered to act immediately, not next month.
Hiring and promoting for adaptability, not tenure. This is the most uncomfortable one, and the one I feel most strongly about. Too many legacy organisations still reward loyalty and institutional memory over the ability to unlearn old habits. The leaders who actually drive transformation are rarely the people who've been doing things "the right way" for fifteen years — they're the ones still curious enough to question it.
A Final Thought
The question is no longer whether to transform. That debate ended years ago, even if some leadership teams haven't admitted it yet.
The real question is whether you'll lead that shift on your own terms — or get forced into it later, after the warning signs have become impossible to ignore and the cost of catching up has multiplied.
Your customers have already left the 2010 playbook behind. The only thing still deciding whether your business survives the next decade is whether you're willing to leave it too.
[ListParagraph] Rethinking What 'Alignment' Actually Means in 2026
The concept hasn't changed. The conditions under which leaders must achieve it have.
Alignment is one of those words that appears in almost every strategy discussion and means something slightly different each time it is used.
Sometimes it refers to agreement on strategic direction. Sometimes it means cascading objectives through an organisation. Sometimes — and this is the most common usage — it describes a state that leadership believes exists but has not actually tested.
I want to revisit the concept, because the conditions under which leaders must build and maintain alignment have shifted significantly, and the traditional approaches are showing their limitations.
What Has Changed
The organisations I work with today are operating in a context that is meaningfully different from even five years ago. Three changes stand out.
The first is structural fragmentation. Hybrid and distributed working patterns have become permanent features of most professional environments. The informal alignment mechanisms that once operated naturally — the conversations between meetings, the shared experience of working in the same physical space — are no longer universally available. Alignment that used to happen organically now has to be engineered.
The second is the pace of strategic change. In many sectors, strategy is no longer a fixed direction set annually and reviewed quarterly. It is a living document, updated in response to market shifts, competitive moves, and — increasingly — the capabilities and limitations of AI tools that are changing what is possible. Maintaining alignment when the strategy itself is in motion requires a different kind of organisational infrastructure.
The third is generational diversity. The workforce now spans multiple generations with meaningfully different expectations about work, leadership, and what it means to be part of an organisation. A concept of alignment built on common goals and shared culture must contend with the reality that people's relationship to those things varies significantly.
Where Traditional Approaches Fall Short
The standard toolkit for building alignment — cascaded objectives, town halls, engagement surveys, leadership communication — was designed for a different context. It assumed a relatively stable strategy, a largely co-located workforce, and a shared baseline of organisational culture.
None of those assumptions reliably hold today. Cascaded objectives can become outdated before they are fully embedded. Town halls can create the appearance of alignment without the reality. Engagement surveys measure how people felt about the past, not how they are positioned to execute the future.
This is not an argument for abandoning these tools. It is an argument for supplementing them with approaches that are better suited to current conditions.

In my experience, the organisations navigating this well share a few common characteristics.
They invest in shared language. Not slogans, but genuine conceptual clarity — a shared understanding of what the organisation means by its most important terms. What does 'customer-first' actually require us to do differently? What does 'agile' mean in our context? This kind of linguistic alignment is unglamorous work, but it significantly reduces the friction that comes from people executing the same strategy in incompatible ways.
They create alignment moments, not just alignment events. A strategy offsite creates alignment for perhaps six weeks. What sustains it is a rhythm of shorter, more frequent conversations — team check-ins, cross-functional problem-solving sessions, informal leadership visibility — that keep the strategic intent alive in daily practice.
They treat misalignment as information. The organisations that catch drift earliest are the ones that have normalised the reporting of it. When a team says they are not sure a priority still applies — that is not a failure of commitment. That is valuable intelligence. Leaders who respond with curiosity rather than defensiveness tend to maintain alignment better over time.
A Final Thought
Alignment, at its best, is not a state that an organisation achieves and then maintains. It is a practice — something that has to be actively tended, recalibrated, and renewed as conditions change.
In 2026, the leaders who understand this — and build organisations capable of continuous realignment rather than periodic resets — will have a meaningful advantage over those who still treat it as a one-time communication challenge.
The concept has not changed. The discipline required to deliver it has.

There is a conversation I have had too many times to count.

This is the knowing-doing gap. And it is one of the most persistent, underacknowledged problems in organisational life.
We Know What Good Looks Like
Most experienced leaders can articulate the principles of effective strategy execution. Focus on a small number of priorities. Translate strategic intent into clear behaviours. Ensure accountability is real, not just declared. Invest in the human side of change.

What I have found, however, is that the same leaders who diagnose these problems in others reproduce them in their own organisations — sometimes within the same year.

Knowing was not enough.
Why Knowledge Doesn't Prevent Repetition
The gap between knowing and doing is not a knowledge problem. It is a pressure problem.
When organisations face performance challenges, the instinct is to act — to add initiatives, accelerate timelines, demonstrate visible effort. The leaders who do this are not naive. They know, at some level, that more activity rarely produces more results. But inaction, or deliberate subtraction, feels like a failure of leadership in the moment.
There is also a social dimension. Strategy discussions in senior teams involve status, relationships, and political capital. Eliminating a priority that a colleague championed is not simply a rational decision — it is a signal. It costs something. And so compromises are made. The agenda stays crowded. The knowing-doing gap widens.
What Breaking the Pattern Actually Requires
In my experience, closing the gap requires three things that feel uncomfortable in practice, even if they sound sensible in theory.
The first is the willingness to subtract publicly. Not quietly deprioritising things, but explicitly naming what the organisation will not do this year — in a town hall, not just a leadership memo. This creates real accountability, because it can be checked.
The second is protecting focus under pressure. The hardest moment in strategy execution is not the launch. It is month four, when results are slower than hoped and the temptation to pivot or add is strongest. The leaders who resist that temptation, and reinforce the original focus instead, tend to be the ones who break through.
The third is honest retrospection. Most organisations conduct post-mortems on failed projects. Very few conduct honest reviews of why they keep repeating the same execution failures. Building that practice — genuinely, not performatively — is one of the highest-return investments a leadership team can make.
A Final Reflection
I no longer believe that leaders fail because they lack insight. Most of the ones I have worked with are perceptive, experienced, and genuinely motivated to get things right.
What trips them up, repeatedly, is the gap between what they know and what the system — the pressure, the politics, the instinct to act — pushes them to do.
Closing that gap is not an intellectual challenge. It is a leadership discipline. And like any discipline, it requires practice, discomfort, and a willingness to do the harder thing when the easier one is available.

In business, leadership is often defined by role, scope, or results. Titles confer authority. Targets validate performance. But neither guarantees legacy.

Whether fairness was consistent or selective
Whether people felt trusted or controlled
Whether success was shared or personalized
Whether dignity was preserved, especially in difficult moments

3. Voluntary Commitment
People give discretionary effort not to systems, but to leaders they respect. Long after formal reporting lines disappear, influence remains.

Short-term wins followed by long-term instability
High talent churn despite strong financials
Cultural resets disguised as “transformations”
In these environments, leaders may be successful on paper, but forgotten—or remembered poorly—once they exit.

[ListParagraph] Most Strategy Problems Are Actually People Problems
Most Strategy Problems Are Actually People Problems
Lessons from Leading a Top-Tier Organization Through Record Performance
Strategy is often discussed as a matter of insight, analysis, and decision-making. In boardrooms and leadership offsites, considerable effort is invested in defining the “right” direction—market positioning, growth ambition, financial targets, and competitive advantage.
Yet in practice, strategy rarely fails because it is intellectually unsound.
It fails because the organization never fully aligns behind it.
This became clear to me during one of my experiences as a General Manager of a top-tier car rental business operating at scale, where I led an organization of approximately 250 people in a highly competitive, execution-driven market.
The mandate was ambitious but clear: deliver record-high revenue performance.
The strategy was robust. The market opportunity existed. Senior leadership support was in place.
What ultimately determined success, however, was not the elegance of the strategy—but how people related to it.
When Strategic Clarity Meets Organizational Reality
At the outset, the organization was facing a familiar leadership challenge. While the overarching objective was widely understood, the business was simultaneously pursuing too many parallel priorities.
Different parts of the organization were optimizing for different outcomes:
Commercial teams were focused on top-line growth
Operations were balancing service quality, utilization, and cost
Support functions were responding to their own functional scorecards
Each priority made sense in isolation. Collectively, they created fragmentation.
This is a common failure mode in strategy execution. Leaders articulate a bold ambition, but do not sufficiently simplify the operating agenda required to deliver it. The result is not resistance, but diffusion—people work hard, yet pull in slightly different directions.
On the surface, progress appears steady. Beneath it, momentum is fragile.
The Hidden Assumption That Undermines Execution
Many leaders assume that once a strategy is approved and communicated, alignment will naturally follow. This assumption is rarely tested—and often incorrect.
People do not execute strategy as it is written.
They execute it as they experience it.
In practice, individuals ask themselves:
What really matters right now?
How will success be judged?
What carries personal or professional risk?
Where should I focus when priorities compete?
If the answers to these questions differ across teams or layers, execution becomes inconsistent—regardless of how clear the strategy appears at the top.
Why Fewer Priorities Matter More Than Better Plans
One of the earliest lessons from this experience was that alignment does not come from adding clarity alone. It comes from removing noise.
Too many initiatives, even well-intentioned ones, weaken focus. They create ambiguity about what “winning” truly means and encourage local optimization over enterprise outcomes.
The leadership challenge, therefore, was not to introduce new direction, but to make deliberate choices about what would not be prioritized at that moment.
Strategy requires subtraction as much as ambition.
The Leadership Shift: From Managing Work to Building Belief
What ultimately unlocked performance was not a change in strategic intent, but a change in leadership emphasis.
The focus shifted toward strengthening:
Team spirit — reinforcing the idea of one organization, not competing silos
Belonging — ensuring people felt included in a shared mission
A collective definition of winning — making success an organizational outcome, not a functional one
Rather than relying solely on targets, dashboards, and pressure, leadership attention moved toward how people felt about the journey—whether they believed they were part of something meaningful and achievable.
This was not about motivation in the abstract. It was about creating the conditions in which people willingly aligned their efforts behind a single objective.
Why Belonging Is a Strategic Advantage
Alignment is often discussed in technical terms—KPIs, governance structures, cascaded objectives. These mechanisms are necessary, but insufficient.
Sustainable execution depends on something deeper: whether people feel connected to one another and to the goal they are pursuing.
When individuals feel part of a winning team:
Decision-making accelerates
Trade-offs become easier
Accountability becomes shared rather than enforced
In such environments, discretionary effort increases—not because people are instructed to do more, but because they want the organization to succeed.
In this case, once the organization began to operate with a genuine sense of shared purpose, execution followed naturally. The strategy itself did not change. The way people related to it did.
The outcome was record performance—achieved not through incremental optimization, but through collective alignment and belief.
The Broader Leadership Insight
Looking back, the most important lesson was this:
Strategy is rarely defeated by poor analysis.
It is defeated by fragmented priorities and weak human alignment.
Leaders who want strategies to succeed must invest as much energy in shaping the organizational experience as they do in shaping plans. Team spirit, belonging, and a shared sense of winning are not “soft” concepts—they are core execution disciplines.
Final Reflection
I no longer believe that strategy succeeds simply because it is well designed.
It succeeds when people feel aligned, included, and genuinely committed to winning together.
When leaders create that environment, strategy has a real chance to become reality

Organizations often attribute underperformance to strategy, market conditions, or execution challenges. Far less frequently do they confront a quieter and more persistent cause: the cumulative cost of getting people decisions wrong.
Hiring is one of the few leadership decisions whose impact compounds every day. A strong hire elevates standards, judgment, and morale over time. A poor hire does the opposite—often subtly at first, but relentlessly.
Yet despite its strategic importance, hiring is frequently rushed, delegated, or treated as a transactional necessity rather than a leadership discipline.

The most effective leaders understand that hiring is not primarily an administrative function. It is a strategic responsibility.
Every hire reshapes the organization in small but meaningful ways:
How decisions are made
How conflict is handled
How priorities are interpreted
How culture is reinforced—or diluted
Once someone enters the system, their influence extends beyond their job description. Over time, they either raise the bar for those around them or quietly lower it.
This is why experienced leaders spend disproportionate time on hiring decisions—particularly for roles that influence others.
Vacancies create pressure. Poor hires create drag.
The Cost of Not Spending Enough Time Upfront
One of the most common leadership mistakes is underestimating the long-term cost of a wrong hire while overestimating the short-term cost of waiting.
Organizations often rush to fill roles because:
Work is piling up
Teams are stretched
Stakeholders are impatient
The result is a decision optimized for speed rather than fit.
What follows is predictable:
Performance requires constant intervention
Accountability becomes blurred
Strong performers compensate for weak ones
Leadership time shifts from progress to damage control
Over time, the organization pays far more in lost momentum than it would have by waiting and hiring correctly.

Not all hiring failures are immediately visible. Some are far more damaging precisely because they persist.
In many organizations, individuals remain in roles despite contributing little real value. They attend meetings, occupy headcount, and appear busy—but their presence creates friction rather than progress.
This kind of misalignment is costly because:
It normalizes mediocrity
It demotivates high performers
It slows decision-making
It erodes trust in leadership judgment
Perhaps most importantly, it sends a signal: contribution is optional.
Left unaddressed, such situations do not remain isolated. They pull others down—particularly those who care deeply about outcomes and fairness.
Why Senior Roles Require Greater Due Diligence
The seniority of a role amplifies its impact. Senior hires do not merely execute—they shape how others execute.
This is why leaders must spend more, not less, time on senior hiring:
Multiple interviews
Deep reference checks
Scenario-based discussions
Values and judgment testing
The cost of getting a senior hire wrong is not limited to their salary. It includes:
Strategic misalignment
Cultural distortion
Loss of credibility
Attrition of strong talent
Patience at this stage is not indecision. It is discipline.
Due Diligence Matters at Every Level
While senior roles demand deeper scrutiny, junior hires are not exempt from due diligence. The criteria, however, should differ.
At junior levels:
Skills can be taught
Systems can be learned
Experience can be built
What cannot be easily taught is:
Mindset
Attitude
Curiosity
Accountability
Willingness to learn
Organizations that hire junior talent based solely on technical skill often discover too late that poor attitude undermines team dynamics far more than skill gaps ever would.
Right mindset scales. Wrong mindset spreads.
Keeping the Right People Motivated and Engaged
Hiring the right people is only the first step. Retention and motivation complete the equation.
Strong performers need:
Clarity of expectations
Fair recognition
Trust and autonomy
A sense of progress and belonging
When the right people feel overlooked or constrained by poor hiring decisions around them, frustration grows. Over time, they disengage—or leave.
Ironically, organizations often work harder to retain average performers than to protect and energize their strongest contributors.
This is a strategic mistake.
Letting Go—Decisively and Respectfully
No discussion of hiring is complete without addressing its most difficult counterpart: letting people go.
When it becomes clear that someone is not right for the role or the organization, delaying the decision rarely improves the outcome. It only prolongs misalignment and increases collective cost.
Handled well, separation can be:
Clear
Respectful
Dignified
Handled poorly, delay creates:
Confusion
Frustration
Resentment
Letting someone go is not a failure of compassion. In many cases, it is an acknowledgment of reality—one that allows both the individual and the organization to move forward.
Decisiveness, when paired with respect, is a leadership responsibility.
The Leadership Discipline Behind Strong Organizations
Strong organizations are not built by chance. They are built by leaders who treat people decisions with the same rigor as financial or strategic ones.
They understand that:
Hiring shapes culture
Culture shapes execution
Execution determines results
They invest time where it matters most—upfront—and act decisively when misalignment becomes clear.
Final Reflection
Strategy sets direction. Processes create structure. But people determine whether anything moves at all.
Organizations that consistently outperform do not do so because they hire faster, cheaper, or more aggressively. They do so because they hire deliberately, develop thoughtfully, and act decisively when fit is no longer present.
The right hire does not guarantee success. But the wrong hire almost always guarantees drag.
Leadership begins—and often ends—with people decisions.

There is a growing tendency to view AI as a corrective force—something that will compensate for human inconsistency, bias, or poor decision-making.

AI materially changes:
Speed — insights and recommendations are generated faster
Scale — patterns emerge across vast datasets
Consistency — processes become more standardized
AI does not change:
Who owns decisions
How trade-offs are made
When judgment overrides data
How trust is built within teams
These remain human responsibilities.
The Risk of Delegating Judgment
The greatest risk in AI adoption is not technical failure. It is the quiet erosion of accountability.

In such environments, AI becomes a shield rather than a tool.

Organizations that benefit most from AI share several characteristics:
Clear decision boundaries
They explicitly define which decisions are automated, which are augmented, and which remain human.
Strong alignment
People understand how AI supports strategy rather than replaces responsibility.
Leadership discipline
Leaders model thoughtful use of AI—neither over-relying on it nor dismissing it.
Psychological safety
Teams are encouraged to question outputs, escalate concerns, and apply judgment when needed.

AI will change how work is done. But strategy, accountability, and leadership will remain human.

[ListParagraph] Leadership in Islam: An Ethical Framework for Modern Leadership
In today’s business and political landscape, leadership is often defined by speed, decisiveness, and results. Yet repeated crises—ethical failures, toxic cultures, and declining trust—suggest that performance alone is not enough. What many modern leadership models struggle to articulate clearly is the moral foundation of authority.
Islamic teachings offer a timeless leadership framework that directly addresses this gap. Far from being limited to religious or political contexts, this framework provides practical guidance for executives, founders, and senior leaders navigating complex organizations and high-stakes decisions.
1. Justice as the Source of Legitimacy
In Islam, leadership is not validated by title or power, but by justice. Justice is not an abstract ideal; it is an operating system. Decisions must be fair, consistent, and free from personal bias.
Translated into organizational leadership, justice means:
Applying performance standards consistently
Avoiding favoritism and informal “exceptions”
Ensuring accountability applies to everyone, including senior leadership
Justice builds credibility. Without it, authority becomes fragile, regardless of results.
2. Integrity and Trust: Leadership’s Hidden Capital
Integrity (amanah) is central to Islamic leadership. Authority is viewed as a trust, not a privilege. Leaders are accountable not only for outcomes, but for how power is exercised.
In modern organizations, integrity:
Reduces the need for excessive controls
Strengthens internal trust
Improves long-term decision quality
Leaders who compromise integrity may gain short-term advantages, but they steadily erode institutional confidence—the most valuable asset any organization possesses.
3. Knowledge, Wisdom, and Consultation
Islamic leadership rejects impulsive, ego-driven decision-making. It emphasizes knowledge, wisdom, and shura—consultation. Leadership is not about having all the answers, but about making informed decisions through collective intelligence.
This principle closely aligns with contemporary best practices:
Complex decisions require data, not intuition alone
Strong leaders invite dissent and expertise
Consultation strengthens outcomes without weakening authority
Wisdom lies not in certainty, but in disciplined judgment.
4. Humility and Compassion: Strength Without Arrogance
Islam places strong emphasis on humility and compassion. Leadership is service, not superiority. Arrogance is viewed as a liability, not a signal of strength.
In organizations, humble leaders:
Create psychological safety
Retain high-performing talent
Encourage accountability without fear
Compassion does not dilute performance standards; it humanizes them.
5. Courage: The Willingness to Stand for What Is Right
Bravery is a defining leadership trait in Islam. Courage is not recklessness—it is the willingness to uphold justice, speak truth, and make difficult decisions when they are unpopular.
In practice, courageous leadership means:
Addressing poor performance decisively
Resisting unethical shortcuts
Making long-term decisions under short-term pressure
Organizations do not fail due to lack of intelligence. They fail when leaders lack courage.
A Leadership Model for Long-Term Value
Islamic leadership principles offer a coherent model where ethics and effectiveness are not competing forces, but mutually reinforcing ones. Justice builds trust. Integrity sustains credibility. Knowledge improves decisions. Humility strengthens teams. Courage protects values.
In a world increasingly skeptical of authority, this framework provides what modern leadership desperately needs: moral clarity paired with practical discipline.
Leadership, in this view, is not about control—it is about responsibility.
