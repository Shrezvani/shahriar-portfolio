Abu Dhabi Just Hit 31,000 Autonomous Rides. The Mobility Industry Hasn’t Noticed Yet.

A balanced assessment of what autonomous vehicles will actually mean for the GCC — and for the businesses that operate here

In the first half of 2026, Abu Dhabi Mobility logged 31,075 rides using self-driving vehicles.

That number will not appear in most mobility industry reviews this year. It will not dominate boardroom agendas at rental companies, fleet operators, or insurance providers across the GCC. It will not prompt urgent strategic reviews at the transport authorities of most regional cities.

It should.

Not because autonomous vehicles are about to replace the regional mobility industry overnight — they are not. But because 31,000 rides is no longer a pilot. It is an operation. And the gap between when a new mobility paradigm becomes an operation and when it begins to reshape incumbent businesses is consistently shorter than those incumbents expect.

I have watched this pattern play out before in this industry. The leaders who engage early shape the transition. The ones who wait for certainty inherit its consequences.

What Is Actually Happening in the GCC

The GCC’s autonomous vehicle programmes are more advanced than most regional business leaders realise, because most of the conversation has stayed within government and technology circles rather than reaching the broader mobility industry.

Abu Dhabi’s programme — built around Waymo-derived technology and coordinated through Abu Dhabi Mobility — has moved from controlled testing to live commercial operation across select urban corridors. Dubai’s Roads and Transport Authority has been running its own autonomous vehicle programme alongside partnerships with international AV operators. The ambition, stated clearly in Dubai’s Smart Mobility Strategy, is for 25 percent of total trips in the emirate to be autonomous by 2030.

These are not aspirational targets from a government strategy deck. They are operating mandates backed by infrastructure investment — dedicated lanes, sensor-equipped roads, regulatory frameworks being quietly built in parallel with the technology itself.

What is missing from this picture is a serious, organised response from the mobility businesses whose models these programmes will eventually affect.

Who Stands to Lose — and How Much

The honest answer is that autonomous vehicles, at scale, represent a structural challenge for several categories of GCC mobility business. The degree of threat varies significantly by segment, and the timeline is longer than headlines suggest — but the direction is clear.

Traditional car rental faces perhaps the most complex strategic question. The core value proposition of car rental — providing a human-driven vehicle for personal use — is not immediately threatened by AVs, which in their early commercial phase are primarily point-to-point urban services rather than multi-day personal transport. But the longer-term pressure is real. If autonomous ride services become genuinely reliable, affordable, and available on demand across GCC cities — which is the trajectory — the casual urban rental occasion begins to erode. Tourists who today rent a car for three days to explore Dubai may increasingly find that an autonomous ride service covers the same need at lower cost and with less friction.

Corporate fleet operators face a different set of questions. Staff transport — airport transfers, inter-office movement, accommodation-to-site commutes — is a significant cost line for many GCC organisations. Autonomous vehicles make economic sense in this context earlier than almost any other application, because routes are predictable, schedules are manageable, and the fleet can be centrally dispatched. Companies running large staff transport operations who are not modelling what AV services will do to their cost base within five years are leaving a material planning gap.

Insurance is the segment where autonomous vehicles will create the most profound structural change, and where the least preparation is currently visible. Motor insurance in the GCC is written almost entirely against human driver behaviour. Liability frameworks, premium models, and claims processes all assume a human at the wheel. When the vehicle is driving itself, the entire liability architecture shifts — from the driver to the manufacturer, the software provider, or the fleet operator. The regional insurance industry has not yet built the products, the actuarial models, or the regulatory relationships required to operate in that environment. The organisations that do this work now will define the market. The ones that wait will be defined by it.

Taxi and ride-hailing operators face the most direct substitution risk, but also the most time to adapt. Careem and Uber are not standing still — both are tracking AV developments and positioning themselves as platform layers that could operate autonomous fleets rather than human-driver networks. The regional question is whether local operators have the capital and the strategic clarity to make a similar transition, or whether they will be absorbed into global platform models as the technology matures.

Who Stands to Win

The disruption framing, while important, is only half the picture. Autonomous vehicles in the GCC will also create significant value — and the organisations positioned to capture it are not all incumbents.

Infrastructure and real estate stand to benefit substantially. Autonomous vehicles require sensor-equipped roads, dedicated operating zones, maintenance depots, and charging infrastructure. The organisations building and managing this infrastructure — whether government entities or private partners — will be essential to the ecosystem regardless of which AV technology ultimately wins the market.

Fleet management and logistics technology providers have an opportunity to become the operational backbone of autonomous mobility in the region. Managing a mixed fleet of autonomous and conventional vehicles, integrating with city traffic systems, and optimising dispatch and utilisation at scale requires software capability that does not yet exist in mature form in the GCC. This is a genuine technology opportunity for regional entrepreneurs and for the established logistics technology players willing to localise.

Tourism and hospitality may benefit more than they expect. An autonomous vehicle network that is reliable, multilingual, and connected to hotel and attraction booking systems could meaningfully improve the visitor experience in a region where navigation, language barriers, and transport coordination are persistent friction points. Hotels, entertainment destinations, and airports that integrate early with AV operators will offer a materially better product than those that don’t.

The Strategic Question for GCC Leaders

The mistake I see most often in established industries facing technological disruption is binary thinking — either dismissing the change as distant and overblown, or panicking about immediate existential threat.

The reality of autonomous vehicles in the GCC is more nuanced than either position. The technology is real and advancing. The government commitment is genuine and well-funded. But the transition will be gradual, uneven across use cases, and shaped significantly by decisions that regional mobility leaders make in the next two to three years.

The organisations that will navigate this best are not necessarily the ones with the most capital or the most advanced technology partnerships. They are the ones that ask the right questions early: which parts of our business are genuinely exposed, and on what timeline? Where does autonomous mobility create an opportunity we are not currently positioned to capture? What capabilities do we need to build — or acquire — to remain relevant as the landscape shifts?

31,000 rides is a signal worth taking seriously. Not as a reason to panic. But as a reason to think harder, plan more deliberately, and move sooner than feels strictly necessary.

In my experience, in this industry and in this region, the organisations that thrive through transition are rarely the ones that moved when they had to.

They are the ones that moved while they still had a choice.
