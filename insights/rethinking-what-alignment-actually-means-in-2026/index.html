Rethinking What 'Alignment' Actually Means in 2026

The concept hasn't changed. The conditions under which leaders must achieve it have.

Alignment is one of those words that appears in almost every strategy discussion and means something slightly different each time it is used.

Sometimes it refers to agreement on strategic direction. Sometimes it means cascading objectives through an organisation. Sometimes — and this is the most common usage — it describes a state that leadership believes exists but has not actually tested.

I want to revisit the concept, because the conditions under which leaders must build and maintain alignment have shifted significantly, and the traditional approaches are showing their limitations.

What Has Changed

The organisations I work with today are operating in a context that is meaningfully different from even five years ago. Three changes stand out.

The first is structural fragmentation. Hybrid and distributed working patterns have become permanent features of most professional environments. The informal alignment mechanisms that once operated naturally — the conversations between meetings, the shared experience of working in the same physical space — are no longer universally available. Alignment that used to happen organically now has to be engineered.

The second is the pace of strategic change. In many sectors, strategy is no longer a fixed direction set annually and reviewed quarterly. It is a living document, updated in response to market shifts, competitive moves, and — increasingly — the capabilities and limitations of AI tools that are changing what is possible. Maintaining alignment when the strategy itself is in motion requires a different kind of organisational infrastructure.

The third is generational diversity. The workforce now spans multiple generations with meaningfully different expectations about work, leadership, and what it means to be part of an organisation. A concept of alignment built on common goals and shared culture must contend with the reality that people's relationship to those things varies significantly.

Where Traditional Approaches Fall Short

The standard toolkit for building alignment — cascaded objectives, town halls, engagement surveys, leadership communication — was designed for a different context. It assumed a relatively stable strategy, a largely co-located workforce, and a shared baseline of organisational culture.

None of those assumptions reliably hold today. Cascaded objectives can become outdated before they are fully embedded. Town halls can create the appearance of alignment without the reality. Engagement surveys measure how people felt about the past, not how they are positioned to execute the future.

This is not an argument for abandoning these tools. It is an argument for supplementing them with approaches that are better suited to current conditions.

What More Effective Alignment Looks Like Now

In my experience, the organisations navigating this well share a few common characteristics.

They invest in shared language. Not slogans, but genuine conceptual clarity — a shared understanding of what the organisation means by its most important terms. What does 'customer-first' actually require us to do differently? What does 'agile' mean in our context? This kind of linguistic alignment is unglamorous work, but it significantly reduces the friction that comes from people executing the same strategy in incompatible ways.

They create alignment moments, not just alignment events. A strategy offsite creates alignment for perhaps six weeks. What sustains it is a rhythm of shorter, more frequent conversations — team check-ins, cross-functional problem-solving sessions, informal leadership visibility — that keep the strategic intent alive in daily practice.

They treat misalignment as information. The organisations that catch drift earliest are the ones that have normalised the reporting of it. When a team says they are not sure a priority still applies — that is not a failure of commitment. That is valuable intelligence. Leaders who respond with curiosity rather than defensiveness tend to maintain alignment better over time.

A Final Thought

Alignment, at its best, is not a state that an organisation achieves and then maintains. It is a practice — something that has to be actively tended, recalibrated, and renewed as conditions change.

In 2026, the leaders who understand this — and build organisations capable of continuous realignment rather than periodic resets — will have a meaningful advantage over those who still treat it as a one-time communication challenge.

The concept has not changed. The discipline required to deliver it has.
