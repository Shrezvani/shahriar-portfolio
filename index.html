On AI

Artificial intelligence is reshaping organizational life. Decision cycles are shortening, information is becoming more accessible, and tasks once dependent on human judgment are increasingly automated. Most discussions focus on capability — what AI can do, how quickly it can be deployed, and where efficiency gains can be captured. Far less attention is paid to the real constraint: people.

There is a growing tendency to view AI as a corrective force — something that will compensate for human inconsistency, bias, or poor decision-making. This is a misunderstanding. AI does not remove ambiguity; it exposes it faster. It does not replace leadership; it demands clearer leadership.

Organizations with weak alignment, unclear decision rights, or poor accountability will not be rescued by technology. They will experience their existing problems at greater speed and scale. That is why my work on AI adoption starts with the operating model — who decides what, on what evidence, and who is accountable for the outcome — before it touches tooling.

What changes, what does not
Changes Speed Insights and recommendations are generated faster.
Changes Scale Patterns emerge across vast datasets.
Changes Consistency Processes become more standardised.
Unchanged Judgment Someone still has to decide what the evidence means.
Unchanged Accountability A model cannot own an outcome.
Unchanged Trust Adoption follows confidence, not capability.
Unchanged Alignment Unclear decision rights get faster, not clearer.

AI will change how organizations work. But people will determine whether it works.
